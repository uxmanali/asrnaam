Lucky Numbers in Muslim Names: How Abjad Values Are Really Calculated

Short answer Ask for a name's "lucky number" on any names site and you will get a digit with no explanation.

Where the numbers come from

Every Arabic letter has carried a numerical value since before Islam; the system is called abjad after its first four letters (alif, ba, jim, dal). Merchants used it for record-keeping, poets for chronograms, and the scholars of Ilm ul Huroof for reading the resonance of words. When a names site prints "lucky number 4", what it has done, knowingly or not, is sum the abjad values of the name's Arabic spelling and reduce the total to a single digit.

The value table

The Mashriqi (Eastern) abjad order assigns: alif 1, ba 2, jim 3, dal 4, ha 5, waw 6, zay 7, ḥa 8, ṭa 9; then ya 10, kaf 20, lam 30, mim 40, nun 50, sin 60, ʿayn 70, fa 80, ṣad 90; then qaf 100, ra 200, shin 300, ta 400, tha 500, kha 600, dhal 700, ḍad 800, ẓa 900, ghayn 1000. Urdu's extra letters (pe, ṭe, che, gaf…) take the value of their parent Arabic form.

The method, in three steps

One: write the name in its Arabic spelling, without vowel marks. Two: sum the letter values. Three: add the digits of the total repeatedly until a single digit remains, this is the "reduced" or lucky number. The intermediate total matters too; classical practitioners worked with the full value, and the single digit is a modern convenience.

Worked examples

Take Muhammad (محمد): mim 40 + ḥa 8 + mim 40 + dal 4 = 92, and 9 + 2 = 11, 1 + 1 = 2. Take Maryam (مریم): mim 40 + ra 200 + ya 10 + mim 40 = 290, reducing to 2. Take Zavian (زفيان): zay 7 + fa 80 + ya 10 + alif 1 + nun 50 = 148, reducing to 4. Every canonical name page on Asrnaam now shows this value in its Quick Facts, computed exactly this way from the name's Arabic spelling, so you can check our arithmetic letter by letter with the table above.

What a lucky number is, and is not

Within the tradition, the number is a summary of the name's letter composition, a fingerprint, not a fortune. Nothing in mainstream Islamic scholarship attaches destiny to it, and Asrnaam presents it as cultural and linguistic heritage: a way of seeing how a name is built.
