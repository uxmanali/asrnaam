R.A Full Form in Islam: What Radi Allahu Anhu Means

R.A stands for Radi Allahu Anhu (رضي الله عنه), an Arabic prayer meaning "may Allah be pleased with him." Muslims write or say it after the name of a Companion of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. For a woman the form is Radi Allahu Anha (may Allah be pleased with her), and for a group it is Radi Allahu Anhum (may Allah be pleased with them).

R.A Full Form in Islam: What Radi Allahu Anhu Means

Short answer R.A stands for Radi Allahu Anhu ( رضي الله عنه ), an Arabic prayer meaning "may Allah be pleased with him."

The exact forms of R.A

The phrase changes with who is being honoured. After one man, Radi Allahu Anhu (رضي الله عنه): Abu Bakr (RA). After one woman, Radi Allahu Anha (رضي الله عنها): Aisha (RA). After two people, Radi Allahu Anhuma (رضي الله عنهما), and after a group, Radi Allahu Anhum (رضي الله عنهم). All four are one prayer conjugated, not four different titles.

Where the phrase comes from

The wording is Quranic. Of the first believers, the Quran says: "Allah is pleased with them, and they are pleased with Him" (radiya Allahu anhum wa radu anhu, 9:100, and again at 98:8). Saying R.A after a Companion's name is repeating what the Quran itself declared about them, which is why the practice is universal across the Muslim world.

R.A compared with the other honorifics

Each abbreviation belongs to a different rank of person, and they are not interchangeable. S.A.W (Sallallahu Alayhi wa Sallam, "Allah's peace and blessings be upon him", written ﷺ) is said only for the Prophet Muhammad. A.S (Alayhis Salam, "peace be upon him") follows the names of the other prophets and of angels: Ibrahim (AS), Jibril (AS). R.A (Radi Allahu Anhu/Anha) follows the Companions: Umar (RA), Khadija (RA). R.H (Rahimahullah, "may Allah have mercy on him") follows respected scholars and elders who came after the Companions: Imam Abu Hanifa (RH). And S.W.T (Subhanahu wa Ta'ala, "glorified and exalted is He") is written only after the name of Allah.

Is it obligatory to say R.A?

Most scholars describe it as a beloved courtesy (mustahabb) rather than an obligation: a habit of respect the community keeps alive. What matters is the intent, honouring those the Quran honoured. Writing the full phrase is considered better than the abbreviation when space allows, though R.A is universally understood in print and online.
