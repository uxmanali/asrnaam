How to Pronounce Muslim Names

Most mispronunciations of Muslim names come down to six Arabic sounds that English simply does not have, plus one habit English speakers bring with them: putting the stress in the wrong place. Learn those two things and the great majority of names become straightforward.

The six sounds English does not have

Arabic distinguishes consonants that English collapses into one. These are the six that cause almost every mispronunciation.

ʿAyn (ع) is a constriction deep in the throat, not a vowel at all, though English speakers usually replace it with one. It opens Ali, Umar, Aisha and Abbas. Say the vowel while tightening the throat as though beginning a cough, and you are close.

Ḥa (ح) is a hard, breathy h from the throat, distinct from the ordinary h of ه. Hasan, Husayn and Hamza all begin with it. English h is too soft; the Arabic sound has friction.

Kha (خ) is the sound at the end of Scottish loch or German Bach. It opens Khadija and Khalid. It is not a k.

Ghayn (غ) is a gargled g, close to the French r in Paris. It appears in Ghazali and Maghrib.

Qaf (ق) is a k produced much further back, near the uvula. Qasim and Qadir use it. Saying an ordinary k is understood everywhere, but the two are different letters.

The emphatics ص ض ط ظ are produced with the tongue flattened and the back raised, which darkens the vowel beside them. This is why the a in Salah sounds deeper than the a in Salam.

Stress: the mistake English speakers make most

English tends to stress the first syllable of an unfamiliar name. Arabic does not work that way. Stress usually falls on a long syllable, and Arabic marks length clearly.

The result is that Haroon is ha-ROON, not HA-roon. Jameel is ja-MEEL. Kareem is ka-REEM. Saeed is sa-EED. Wherever you see a doubled English vowel in a transliteration (oo, ee, aa), that is a long Arabic vowel, and the stress almost always lands there.

Long vowels are not decoration

Arabic has three vowels, each with a short and a long form, and the difference changes the word. Holding the vowel roughly twice as long is not optional emphasis, it is part of the name. This is why Aamir and Amir are written differently in English: the first has a long opening vowel, the second does not.

Why the same name sounds different in different countries

A name written one way in Arabic can be said several ways, and all of them can be correct within their tradition.

In Egypt, ج is a hard g, so Jamal is said Gamal. In the Gulf and the Levant it stays a j. In Persian and Urdu, و is often a v rather than a w, which is why the same name appears as Anwar in Arabic and Anvar in Central Asia. Turkish drops Arabic throat sounds entirely and respells the result, giving Zehra for Zahra and Osman for Uthman.

None of these is a corruption. They are the name as it settled into a living language.

A quick method for any name

Find the long vowel and stress it. Check whether the first letter is one of the six sounds above. Give the emphatic letters a darker vowel. Then say it at normal speed rather than carefully, because over-articulating is its own kind of error.

Every name page on AsrNaam carries a stress-marked pronunciation and IPA alongside the Arabic spelling. Start with Haroon, Seraj, Aisha or Khadija, or browse all names.

Asking is better than guessing

A name is the first thing a person owns and the thing they hear most often. Asking someone how they say their own name, and then using it that way, costs a moment and communicates something that no amount of careful study does on its own.

Frequently asked questions

How do you pronounce the Arabic letter ayn?

Ayn (ع) is a constriction in the throat rather than a vowel. Produce the vowel while tightening the throat, as if starting a cough. It opens names such as Ali, Umar and Aisha.

Where does the stress go in Arabic names?

Usually on the long syllable. Where an English transliteration doubles a vowel (oo, ee, aa) that marks a long Arabic vowel and normally takes the stress: ha-ROON, ja-MEEL, ka-REEM.

Why is Jamal pronounced Gamal in Egypt?

Egyptian Arabic pronounces the letter jim (ج) as a hard g, while most other dialects keep it as j. Both are correct within their own tradition.

Is it wrong to pronounce qaf as a normal k?

It will be understood everywhere, but qaf (ق) and kaf (ك) are separate letters in Arabic. Qaf is produced further back in the mouth, near the uvula.
