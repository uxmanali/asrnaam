Arabic girl names beginning with A

The English letter A has no single Arabic counterpart. The 157 names on this page open with 3 different Arabic letters, and in the Science of Letters each of those carries a different quality, element and divine name. Two names that both look like A in English can begin very differently once they are written in Arabic.

  • عAin opens 70 of them, among them Aisha, Ayra, Aliya.
  • أHamza opens 47 of them, among them Anabia, Ayaana, Amaya.
  • اAlif opens 39 of them, among them Ayzal, Ayla, Afreen.

Questions about Muslim names starting with A

How many Muslim girl names start with A?
AsrNaam lists 157 of them. Across boys and girls together the letter A accounts for 431 researched names, 274 for boys and 157 for girls. Every one has its Arabic spelling, its meaning, and its letter-by-letter Ilm ul Huroof reading.
What Arabic letter does A correspond to?
There is no single answer, which is what makes this letter interesting. Most often A stands for Ain (ع), which opens 70 of these names. It can also stand for Hamza and Alif. Each of those Arabic letters has its own element and divine name in the Science of Letters, so two names that look alike in English can read very differently.
Which Muslim girl name starting with A is most popular?
By what visitors actually search for and open on this site, Aisha, Ayra and Ayzal lead the letter A. The settled meaning of Aisha is: Living, lively, prosperous, Mother of the Believers, wife of the Prophet. Popularity is worth knowing, but the tradition weighs meaning first.
Are there Quranic names beginning with A?
Yes. 5 of the names on this page appear in the Quran or belong to a figure named in it, among them Aisha, Aishah and Aminah. A Quranic name is not required for a name to be Islamic, but many parents look for one.
What is the Ilm ul Huroof reading of a name starting with A?
Open any name on this page and you will find it: the name broken into its Arabic letters, each letter with its element, its abjad value and the divine name it is associated with, then a reading of the whole. It is a tradition for reflection, not for prediction.
