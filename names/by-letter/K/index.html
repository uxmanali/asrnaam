Arabic baby names beginning with K

The English letter K has no single Arabic counterpart. The 92 names on this page open with 4 different Arabic letters, and in the Science of Letters each of those carries a different quality, element and divine name. Two names that both look like K in English can begin very differently once they are written in Arabic.

  • كKaf opens 53 of them, among them Kulsoom, Kian, Kenan.
  • خKha opens 31 of them, among them Khadija, Khadijat, Khalid.
  • قQaf opens 5 of them, among them Kanat, Kairo, Kadir.
  • اAlif opens 2 of them, among them Khansa, Khidr.

Questions about Muslim names starting with K

How many Muslim baby names start with K?
AsrNaam lists 92 of them. Across boys and girls together the letter K accounts for 92 researched names, 56 for boys and 37 for girls. Every one has its Arabic spelling, its meaning, and its letter-by-letter Ilm ul Huroof reading.
What Arabic letter does K correspond to?
There is no single answer, which is what makes this letter interesting. Most often K stands for Kaf (ك), which opens 53 of these names. It can also stand for Kha, Qaf and Alif. Each of those Arabic letters has its own element and divine name in the Science of Letters, so two names that look alike in English can read very differently.
Which Muslim baby name starting with K is most popular?
By what visitors actually search for and open on this site, Kulsoom, Kian and Kenan lead the letter K. The settled meaning of Kulsoom is: Full-cheeked. Popularity is worth knowing, but the tradition weighs meaning first.
Are there Quranic names beginning with K?
Yes. 5 of the names on this page appear in the Quran or belong to a figure named in it, among them Kawthar, Khadija and Khadijah. A Quranic name is not required for a name to be Islamic, but many parents look for one.
What is the Ilm ul Huroof reading of a name starting with K?
Open any name on this page and you will find it: the name broken into its Arabic letters, each letter with its element, its abjad value and the divine name it is associated with, then a reading of the whole. It is a tradition for reflection, not for prediction.
